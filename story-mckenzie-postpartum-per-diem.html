Postpartum Per Diem: McKenzie's Story

McKenzie works evenings as a per diem postpartum nurse and plans to become an outpatient women's health NP. Here is her work, in her own words.

Could you see yourself here?

Nurse for
About 3.5 years
Schedule
Evenings, about 3 to 11:30 p.m., per diem
Minimum
4 shifts per month
Patients
Usually four couplets, so eight patients
Plans
Outpatient women's health NP

How long have you been a nurse?

About 3.5 years. My first job was post partum per diem night shift in Oregon. Then I moved to the California in 2020.

What made you decide to accept this current job?

I loved women's health and babies, so I went into nursing school planning to be a midwife. Then I did my L&D rotation and thought, that’s not for me. Postpartum felt like a little oasis compared to the other rotations. I plan to become an outpatient women's health NP, so I'm doing per diem in the meantime. I love it because I'm choosing to be there each day I work.

What schedule do you work?

Evenings, about 3 to 11:30 p.m., per diem. I love it. Compared with my old job, I’m exponentially happier. As a per diem nurse, I must sign up to a minimum of 4 shifts per month. Most of the time there’s a need, so I can pick up shifts whenever I want, or someone will cover me if I can’t find anyone. It’s a mental change: “I’m choosing to be here today.” When I get several days off in a row, I can travel without stressing about coverage, and I’m more rejuvenated even if I’m just as busy. I think it makes me a better nurse. At my old job, nobody got more than two days off in a row. It wasn’t policy, it was just how they’d managed schedules for years. If you wanted a five-day trip, you had to practically trade your firstborn to get coverage.

What does a typical shift look like?

The first hour is the most chaotic. I go to each room, ask if they need anything, then start assessments: fundus, breasts, DVT check, head to toe, vitals, then the baby’s vitals and head to toe. They’re asking lots of questions the whole time. If it’s feeding time, that’s when they ask me to help with latching. With four patients, the fastest I can finish is about 1.5 hours. With lactation or high blood pressure it can be almost nonstop until 5:30 or 6. I don’t pride myself on getting in and out. The point is to know my patients well enough that nothing surprises me later.

With a low-acuity couplet, I feel like a waitress, responding to calls for water or food. But I’m also charting on eight people, which takes a lot of time.

The last part of my shift is mostly admissions and discharges, which can go until 9 or even 10 p.m. Some people are so eager to go home. There are also the 24-hour checks for babies, which include jaundice levels, newborn screens, and weight loss, and those can happen at any time. Discharge means a ton of teaching, and that’s the moment parents say “wait, we’re going home?” You can see it in their eyes.

How many patients do you get in a shift (on average)?

Usually four couplets, so eight patients. Four are crying all the time, and four are crying some of the time. And the dads count too. A lot of it is managing partner and family dynamics, so sometimes it feels like being a therapist. I actually like that part.

What are the best parts of this specialty, and what is the hardest part?

Watching parents go from clueless to capable. I love teaching dads to swaddle, and when I come back they say “how does it look?” and it’s a perfect little burrito. It’s more small moments than one big one. I also believe newborns feed off your energy. When I give the newborn injections, I have a family member touch and talk to the baby, and it goes better. I do skin-to-skin with every blood sugar check.

Lactation takes the most time, easily 30 to 40 minutes with one person, and it’s not shown on the assignment board. Preeclampsia or twins are on the assignment board, but a mom who’s struggling to feed isn’t. I think it should be ranked as a real condition.

My least favorite part is when I’m stripped of resources and can’t be the nurse I want to be because I have 12 other things going on. It only takes one time of a mom feeling unsupported with feeding for her to say “I’m over it, just give the bottle.”

What would you tell a new nurse about the problems they’ll run into?

Medically, hemorrhages are the biggest thing, and it’s hard to really know until you’re in it, so good drills help. Also timing and staffing, and accepting that you won’t give every patient the experience they want. It’s cliché, but it took me a long time to let go of the idea that I had to be perfect in every room. And it’s hard to have sick babies. Postpartum is mostly a happy place, but there’s risk anywhere. You may also have a mom with a fetal demise. It is more common than I thought and I wish we talked about it more.

How do you cope with the heavy parts of the job?

I compartmentalize to an extent, and I try to leave it at work. I don’t go fast through rooms. I stay, ask if they have questions, and show them they’re seen as a human being, even if their experience isn’t what they imagined. That makes me less stressed in the long run. Outside of work, physical activity is huge for me. A lot of my friends aren’t in healthcare, so I don’t talk about it much outside. And I travel a lot, like to Hawaii.

How does this job support your work-life balance, and in what ways does it not?

Per diem lets me choose my days, and I get several days off in a row, so I can travel and I feel more rejuvenated, even if I'm equally busy. Exercise is my decompressor, and most of my friends aren't in healthcare. The downside is health insurance. If I could do per diem forever without needing it, I would never go back.
